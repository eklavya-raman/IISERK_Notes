2 Differentiability

We have already defined the concept of continuity of a function at a point and on an interval. Now we will define the concept of differentiability of a function at a point and on an interval. Differentiability is a stronger condition than continuity, as it implies that the function is not only continuous but also has a well-defined tangent line at every point in its domain. In other words, if a function is differentiable at a point, then it must be continuous at that point, but the converse is not necessarily true. For example, the function f(x)=|x|f(x) = |x| is continuous at x=0x = 0 but not differentiable at x=0x = 0.

2.1 Derivative of a function

Let us consider a function f:I→ℝf: I \to \mathbb{R}. We want to define the derivative of ff at a point x0∈Ix_0 \in I. Intuitively, the derivative represents the rate of change of the function at that point. We can think of the derivative as the slope of the tangent line to the graph of the function at the point x0x_0. Let us now formalize this intuition.

Definition 2.1.1 (Derivative)

Let f:I→ℝf: I \to \mathbb{R} be a function and let x0∈Ix_0 \in I. We say that ff is differentiable at x0x_0 if the following limit exists: f′(x0)=limh→0f(x0+h)−f(x0)h.f'(x_0) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(x_0 + h) - f(x_0)}{h}. If this limit exists, we call it the derivative of ff at x0x_0 and denote it by f′(x0)f'(x_0) or dfdx(x0)\frac{df}{dx}(x_0). For endpoints of the interval II, we can define the derivative using one-sided limits. If x0x_0 is the left endpoint of II, we define the derivative as f′(x0)=limh→0+f(x0+h)−f(x0)h.f'(x_0) = \lim_{h \to 0^+} \frac{f(x_0 + h) - f(x_0)}{h}. If x0x_0 is the right endpoint of II, we define the derivative as f′(x0)=limh→0−f(x0+h)−f(x0)h.f'(x_0) = \lim_{h \to 0^-} \frac{f(x_0 + h) - f(x_0)}{h}. Hence, the derivative is well defined at every point in the interior of II and at the endpoints of II as long as the appropriate one-sided limits exist.

We see that it is not always the case that the derivative of a function exists at every point. For example, consider the function f(x)=|x|f(x) = |x|. The derivative of ff at x=0x = 0 does not exist because the limit from the left and the limit from the right do not agree. However, the derivative of ff exists at every other point. For x>0x > 0, we have f′(x)=1f'(x) = 1 and for x<0x < 0, we have f′(x)=−1f'(x) = -1. This illustrates the fact that a continuous function may not be differentiable at every point. In fact, there are many examples of continuous functions that are not differentiable at any point, such as the Weierstrass function.

Definition 2.1.2 (Differentiability)

A function f:I→ℝf: I \to \mathbb{R} is said to be differentiable on an point x0∈Ix_0 \in I if the derivative of ff at x0x_0 exists. If ff is differentiable at every point in an interval II, we say that ff is differentiable on II.

Note 2.1.1

Linearity of the derivative: If ff and gg are differentiable at x0x_0, then for any constants a,b∈ℝa, b \in \mathbb{R}, the function h=af+bgh = af + bg is also differentiable at x0x_0 and we have h′(x0)=af′(x0)+bg′(x0).h'(x_0) = a f'(x_0) + b g'(x_0). We can view the derivative as a linear operator from C1(I)C^1(I) to the space of all functions on II (denoted by C(I)C(I)). This means that the derivative satisfies the properties of linearity, such as additivity and homogeneity. In particular, if we have two differentiable functions ff and gg, and a constant cc, we have: (f+g)′(x)=f′(x)+g′(x),(cf)′(x)=cf′(x).\begin{align*} (f + g)'(x) &= f'(x) + g'(x), \\ (cf)'(x) &= c f'(x). \end{align*} If we consider the space of all infinitely differentiable functions on II. (denoted by C∞(I)C^\infty(I)), we can view the derivative as a linear operator from C∞(I)C^\infty(I) to itself. This has important implications in the study of differential equations and functional analysis, as it allows us to apply linear algebraic techniques to the study of differentiable functions.

2.2 Algebra of Differentiable Functions

As mentioned earlier, the derivative is a linear operator. This means that if we have two differentiable functions ff and gg, and a constant cc, we have:

  • (f+g)′(x)=f′(x)+g′(x)(f + g)'(x) = f'(x) + g'(x),

  • (cf)′(x)=cf′(x)(cf)'(x) = c f'(x),

Now, let us consider the application of derivatives in optimization problems. Optimization problems are of the form - max⁡f(x)\max f(x) or min⁡f(x)\min f(x) subject to some constraints. The function ff is called the objective function and the constraints are usually given in the form of inequalities or equalities. The goal is to find the value of xx that maximizes or minimizes the objective function while satisfying the constraints. Let us define what it means for a point to a maximum or minimum of a function.

Definition 2.2.1 (Global Maximum and Minimum)

Let f:I→ℝf: I \to \mathbb{R} be a function. We say that ff has a global maximum at x0∈Ix_0 \in I if f(x0)≥f(x)f(x_0) \geq f(x) for all x∈Ix \in I. Similarly, we say that ff has a global minimum at x0∈Ix_0 \in I if f(x0)≤f(x)f(x_0) \leq f(x) for all x∈Ix \in I.

We can also define the concept of local maximum and minimum, which is a weaker condition than global maximum and minimum.

Definition 2.2.2 (Local Maximum and Minimum)

Let f:I→ℝf: I \to \mathbb{R} be a function. We say that ff has a local maximum at x0∈Ix_0 \in I if there exists an interval (x0−δ,x0+δ)⊆I(x_0 - \delta, x_0 + \delta) \subseteq I such that f(x0)≥f(x)f(x_0) \geq f(x) for all x∈(x0−δ,x0+δ)x \in (x_0 - \delta, x_0 + \delta). Similarly, we say that ff has a local minimum at x0∈Ix_0 \in I if there exists an interval (x0−δ,x0+δ)⊆I(x_0 - \delta, x_0 + \delta) \subseteq I such that f(x0)≤f(x)f(x_0) \leq f(x) for all x∈(x0−δ,x0+δ)x \in (x_0 - \delta, x_0 + \delta).

Let us prove some theorems that give us a more concrete understanding of how functions behave at and around their local maxima and minima. The first theorem is the following:

Theorem 2.2.2 (Interior Extremum Theorem)

Let f:I→ℝf: I \to \mathbb{R} be a function and let x0∈Ix_0 \in I be an interior point of II. If ff has a local maximum or minimum at x0x_0, then f′(x0)=0f'(x_0) = 0.

Notice that points where the derivatives are zero are where the function has a local maximum or minimum. However, it is not always the case that if the derivative of a function is zero at a point, then the function has a local maximum or minimum at that point. For example, consider the function f(x)=x3f(x) = x^3. The derivative of ff at x=0x = 0 is zero, but ff does not have a local maximum or minimum at x=0x = 0. Therefore, we need to define the concept of critical points to capture these points where the derivative is zero but there is no local extremum.

Definition 2.2.3 (Critical Point)

Let f:I→ℝf: I \to \mathbb{R} be a differentiable function. A point x0∈Ix_0 \in I is called a critical point of ff if either f′(x0)=0f'(x_0) = 0 or f′(x0)f'(x_0) does not exist.

Now, we need to determine whether a critical point exists in a given interval. The following theorem gives us a sufficient condition for the existence of a critical point in an interval.

Theorem 2.2.3 (Rolle’s Theorem)

Let f:[a,b]→ℝf: [a, b] \to \mathbb{R} be a continuous function on [a,b][a, b] and differentiable on (a,b)(a, b). If f(a)=f(b)f(a) = f(b), then there exists a point c∈(a,b)c \in (a, b) such that f′(c)=0f'(c) = 0.

2.3 Higher Order Derivatives

We can also define higher order derivatives of a function, that is applying the derivative operator multiple times to a function. The second derivative of a function ff is denoted by f″f'' and is defined as the derivative of the first derivative f′f'. Similarly, the nn-th derivative of a function ff is denoted by f(n)f^{(n)} and is defined as the derivative of the (n−1)(n-1)-th derivative f(n−1)f^{(n-1)}. Higher order derivatives are useful in analyzing the behavior of functions and in solving differential equations.

Proof

3 Integration

Now, we have a good understanding of the properties of derivatives and how they can be used to analyze the behavior of functions. Let us now discover how to reverse the process of differentiation, that is, how to find a function given its derivative. This process is called integration and it is a fundamental concept in analysis. We will define the concept of the integral and prove some theorems concerning the properties of integrals and how they can be used to analyze the behavior of functions.

Definition 3.1 (Riemann Integral)

Let f:[a,b]→ℝf: [a, b] \to \mathbb{R} be a function. We say that ff is Riemann integrable on [a,b][a, b] if the limit ∫abf(x)dx=limn→∞∑k=1n−1f(a+kb−an)⋅b−an\int_a^b f(x) dx = \lim_{n \to \infty} \sum_{k=1}^{n-1} f(a + k\frac{b-a}{n}) \cdot \frac{b-a}{n} exists. The limit ∫abf(x)dx\int_a^b f(x) dx is called the Riemann integral of ff on [a,b][a, b].

It is useful to have a geometric intuition for this sum. We can think of the Riemann integral as the area under the curve of the function ff from aa to bb. The sum ∑k=1n−1f(a+kb−an)⋅b−an\sum_{k=1}^{n-1} f(a + k\frac{b-a}{n}) \cdot \frac{b-a}{n} can be thought of as the area of a rectangle with height f(a+kb−an)f(a + k\frac{b-a}{n}) and width b−an\frac{b-a}{n}. As nn increases, the rectangles become thinner and the sum approaches the area under the curve of ff from aa to bb.

We now provide a more solid base for the concept of integration. First, we provide the definition of a partition of an interval, which is a crucial concept in the definition of the Riemann integral.

Definition 3.2 (Partition of an Interval)

Let [a,b][a, b] be an interval. A partition of [a,b][a, b] is a finite set of points {x0,x1,…,xn}\{x_0, x_1, \ldots, x_n\} such that a=x0<x1<⋯<xn=ba = x_0 < x_1 < \cdots < x_n = b. The points x0,x1,…,xnx_0, x_1, \ldots, x_n are called the partition points and the intervals [xk−1,xk][x_{k-1}, x_k] for k=1,2,…,nk = 1, 2, \ldots, n are called the subintervals of the partition.

We now define the refinements of a partition, which are used to define the upper and lower sums of a function with respect to a partition.

Definition 3.3 (Refinement of a Partition)

Let P={x0,x1,…,xn}P = \{x_0, x_1, \ldots, x_n\} be a partition of [a,b][a, b]. A refinement of PP is a partition Q={y0,y1,…,ym}Q = \{y_0, y_1, \ldots, y_m\} such that P⊆QP \subseteq Q. In other words, every partition point of PP is also a partition point of QQ.

We now define the upper and lower sums of a function with respect to a partition, which are used to define the Riemann integral.

Definition 3.4 (Upper and Lower Sums)

Let f:[a,b]→ℝf: [a, b] \to \mathbb{R} be a function and let P={x0,x1,…,xn}P = \{x_0, x_1, \ldots, x_n\} be a partition of [a,b][a, b]. The upper sum of ff with respect to PP is defined as U(f,P)=∑k=1nMk(xk−xk−1)U(f, P) = \sum_{k=1}^n M_k (x_k - x_{k-1}) where Mk=sup⁡{f(x):x∈[xk−1,xk]}M_k = \sup\{f(x) : x \in [x_{k-1}, x_k]\} for k=1,2,…,nk = 1, 2, \ldots, n. The lower sum of ff with respect to PP is defined as L(f,P)=∑k=1nmk(xk−xk−1)L(f, P) = \sum_{k=1}^n m_k (x_k - x_{k-1}) where mk=inf⁡{f(x):x∈[xk−1,xk]}m_k = \inf\{f(x) : x \in [x_{k-1}, x_k]\} for k=1,2,…,nk = 1, 2, \ldots, n.

Note that U(f,P)≥L(f,P)U(f, P) \geq L(f, P) for all partitions PP of [a,b][a, b]. This is because Mk≥mkM_k \geq m_k for all k=1,2,…,nk = 1, 2, \ldots, n.
Also, if QQ is a refinement of PP, then we have U(f,Q)≤U(f,P)U(f, Q) \leq U(f, P) and L(f,Q)≥L(f,P)L(f, Q) \geq L(f, P). This is because the supremum of a set is greater than or equal to the supremum of any subset and the infimum of a set is less than or equal to the infimum of any subset.
