1 Rings, Ideals and Fields

1.1 Basic Definitions

1.1.1 Rings

We discussed the algebraic structure of groups in the previous course. In this course, we will discuss a different algebraic structure called a ring -

Definition 1.1.1.1 (Ring)

A ring is a set RR equipped with two binary operations ++ (addition) and ⋅\cdot (multiplication) such that:

  1. (R,+)(R, +) is an abelian group.

    1. There exists an element 0∈R0 \in R such that for all a∈Ra \in R, we have a+0=aa + 0 = a. [Existence of additive identity]

    2. For each a∈Ra \in R, there exists an element −a∈R-a \in R such that a+(−a)=0a + (-a) = 0. [Existence of additive inverse]

    3. For all a,b,c∈Ra, b, c \in R, we have (a+b)+c=a+(b+c)(a + b) + c = a + (b + c). [Associativity of addition]

    4. For all a,b∈Ra, b \in R, we have a+b=b+aa + b = b + a. [Commutativity of addition]

    5. For all a,b∈Ra, b \in R, we have a+b∈Ra + b \in R. [Closure of addition]

  2. (R,⋅)(R, \cdot) is a semigroup.

    1. For all a,b,c∈Ra, b, c \in R, we have (a⋅b)⋅c=a⋅(b⋅c)(a \cdot b) \cdot c = a \cdot (b \cdot c). [Associativity of multiplication]

    2. For all a,b∈Ra, b \in R, we have a⋅b∈Ra \cdot b \in R. [Closure of multiplication]

  3. Multiplication distributes over addition: for all a,b,c∈Ra, b, c \in R, we have a⋅(b+c)=a⋅b+a⋅ca \cdot (b + c) = a \cdot b + a \cdot c and (a+b)⋅c=a⋅c+b⋅c(a + b) \cdot c = a \cdot c + b \cdot c.

  4. There exists an element 1∈R1 \in R such that for all a∈Ra \in R, we have a⋅1=a=1⋅aa \cdot 1 = a = 1 \cdot a. [Existence of multiplicative identity]

A natural question is - Is the multiplicative identity unique? The answer is yes, and we can prove it as follows -

Theorem 1.1.1.1 (Uniqueness of Multiplicative Identity)

Let RR be a ring. Then the multiplicative identity in RR is unique.

Proof

Let 11 and 1′1' be two multiplicative identities in RR. Then we have 1⋅1′=1′1 \cdot 1' = 1' and 1⋅1′=11 \cdot 1' = 1. Therefore, we have 1=1′1 = 1'. Hence, the multiplicative identity is unique. ◻

Clearly, this is a richer structure than a group. In fact, we can see that a ring is a group with an additional operation of multiplication that satisfies certain properties. We now give some examples of rings -

Example 1.1.1.1

The set of integers ℤ\mathbb{Z} with the usual addition and multiplication operations forms a ring. We can verify that all the properties of a ring are satisfied. The additive identity is 00, the additive inverse of an integer aa is −a-a, with addition being commutative and the multiplicative identity is 11. The distributive property also holds for integers. Other examples of rings include the set of rational numbers ℚ\mathbb{Q}, the set of real numbers ℝ\mathbb{R}, and the set of complex numbers ℂ\mathbb{C}, all with the usual addition and multiplication operations.

Note 1.1.1.1

Depending on whether the multiplication operation is commutative or not, we can classify rings into two types - commutative rings and non-commutative rings. A ring RR is said to be a commutative ring if for all a,b∈Ra, b \in R, we have a⋅b=b⋅aa \cdot b = b \cdot a. Otherwise, it is called a non-commutative ring. For example, the set of integers ℤ\mathbb{Z} is a commutative ring, while M2(ℝ)M_2(\mathbb{R}) (the set of 2×22 \times 2 matrices with real entries) is a non-commutative ring.

A useful non-example of a ring is GLn(ℝ)GL_n(\mathbb{R}), the set of all invertible n×nn \times n matrices with real entries. This is not a ring because it does not contain the zero matrix, which is the additive identity.

1.1.2 Ideals

We now define special subsets of rings called ideals -

Definition 1.1.2.1 (Ideal)

An ideal of a ring RR is a subset I⊆RI \subseteq R such that:

  1. II is an abelian subgroup of (R,+)(R, +).

  2. For all r∈Rr \in R and a∈Ia \in I, we have r⋅a∈Ir \cdot a \in I. [Absorption property]

Note 1.1.2.1

Note that the ideal defined above is a left ideal. We can similarly define a right ideal by replacing the absorption property with the following - For all r∈Rr \in R and a∈Ia \in I, we have a⋅r∈Ia \cdot r \in I. If a subset II of a ring RR is both a left ideal and a right ideal, then it is called a both-sided ideal.

We now look at a specific type of ideal called a principal ideal -

Definition 1.1.2.2 (Principal Ideal)

Let RR be a ring and a∈Ra \in R. The principal ideal generated by aa is the set (a)={r⋅a∣r∈R}(a) = \{r \cdot a \mid r \in R\}. A principal ideal is an ideal that can be generated by a single element.

Theorem 1.1.2.1 (Principle Ideal is an Ideal)

The principal ideal (a)(a) generated by an element a∈Ra \in R is an ideal of the ring RR.

Proof

We need to verify whether the principal ideal (a)(a) is indeed an ideal. We need to check whether (a)(a) is an abelian subgroup of (R,+)(R, +) and whether it satisfies the absorption property.

  1. To show that (a)(a) is an abelian subgroup, it is sufficient to show that (a)(a) is closed under addition and additive inverses. Let r1,r2∈Rr_1, r_2 \in R. Then we have r1⋅a+r2⋅a=(r1+r2)⋅a∈(a)r_1 \cdot a + r_2 \cdot a = (r_1 + r_2) \cdot a \in (a). Therefore, (a)(a) is closed under addition. Also, we have −(r⋅a)=(−r)⋅a∈(a)-(r \cdot a) = (-r) \cdot a \in (a). Therefore, (a)(a) is closed under additive inverses. Hence, (a)(a) is an abelian subgroup of (R,+)(R, +).

  2. To show that (a)(a) satisfies the absorption property, let r∈Rr \in R and s⋅a∈(a)s \cdot a \in (a). Then we have r⋅(s⋅a)=(r⋅s)⋅a∈(a)r \cdot (s \cdot a) = (r \cdot s) \cdot a \in (a). Therefore, (a)(a) satisfies the absorption property.
