1.1 Basic Definitions and Concepts

1.1.1 Sample Spaces and Random Experiments

1.1.1.1 Definitions

Definition 1.1.1.1 (Random Experiment)

A random experiment is a process or action that leads to one or more outcomes that cannot be predicted with certainty. The conditions for a experiment to be classified as a random experiment are:

  1. All possible outcomes of the experiment must be known in advance.

  2. The experiment must be repeatable under the same conditions.

  3. The outcome of the experiment must be uncertain, that is we cannot predict its outcome with certainty.

Example 1.1.1.1

The classic example of a random experiment is the tossing of a fair coin. The possible outcomes are heads or tails, and we cannot predict with certainty which outcome will occur on any given toss. The experiment can be repeated multiple times under the same conditions.

Example 1.1.1.2

Another example of a random experiment is rolling a fair six-sided die. The possible outcomes are the numbers 1 through 6, and we cannot predict with certainty which number will appear on any given roll. The experiment can be repeated multiple times under the same conditions.

Example 1.1.1.3

A third example of a random experiment is drawing a card from a standard deck of 52 playing cards. The possible outcomes are the 52 individual cards, and we cannot predict with certainty which card will be drawn. The experiment can be repeated multiple times under the same conditions.

Definition 1.1.1.2 (Sample Space)

The sample space is the set of all possible outcomes of a random experiment. We denote the sample space by the symbol SS or Ω\Omega.

Example 1.1.1.4

Consider the random experiment of tossing a fair coin. The sample space for this experiment is S={H,T}S = \{H, T\}, where HH represents heads and TT represents tails.

Example 1.1.1.5

Consider the random experiment of rolling a fair six-sided die. The sample space for this experiment is S={1,2,3,4,5,6}S = \{1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6\}.

Example 1.1.1.6

Consider the random experiment of drawing a card from a standard deck of 52 playing cards. The sample space for this experiment is S={A♡,2♡,…,K♠}S = \{A\heartsuit, 2\heartsuit, \ldots, K\spadesuit\}.

Definition 1.1.1.3 (Event)

An event is a subset of the sample space. It can consist of one or more outcomes. We denote an event by a capital letter, such as AA, BB, or CC.

Example 1.1.1.7

Consider the random experiment of rolling a fair six-sided die. The sample space for this experiment is S={1,2,3,4,5,6}S = \{1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6\}. An example of an event could be A={2,4,6}A = \{2, 4, 6\}, which represents the event of rolling an even number.

Example 1.1.1.8

Consider the random experiment of drawing a card from a standard deck of 52 playing cards. The sample space for this experiment is S={A♡,2♡,…,K♠}S = \{A\heartsuit, 2\heartsuit, \ldots, K\spadesuit\}. An example of an event could be B={A♡,2♡,3♡}B = \{A\heartsuit, 2\heartsuit, 3\heartsuit\}, which represents the event of drawing a heart with a value of 1, 2, or 3.

Definition 1.1.1.4 (Exhaustive Sample Space)

A sample space is said to be exhaustive if it includes all possible outcomes of a random experiment.

Example 1.1.1.9

Consider the random experiment of rolling a fair six-sided die. The sample space for this experiment is S={1,2,3,4,5,6}S = \{1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6\}. This sample space is exhaustive because it includes all possible outcomes of the experiment.

Definition 1.1.1.5 (Equilikely Events and Mutually Exclusive Events)

Equilikely Events: Two or more events are said to be equilikely if they have the same probability of occurring. For example, when rolling a fair six-sided die, the events of rolling a 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, or 6 are all equilikely because each event has a probability of 16\frac{1}{6}.
Mutually Exclusive Events: Two or more events are said to be mutually exclusive if they cannot occur at the same time. For example, when rolling a fair six-sided die, the events of rolling a 1 and rolling a 2 are mutually exclusive because both outcomes cannot happen simultaneously. In set theoretic notation, we can define mutually exclusive events as follows:
A∩B=∅A \cap B = \emptyset

We give a few examples of mutually exclusive events and events which are not below:

Example 1.1.1.10

Consider the random experiment of rolling a fair six-sided die. The events of rolling a 1 and rolling a 2 are mutually exclusive because both outcomes cannot happen simultaneously.

Example 1.1.1.11

Consider the example of events which are not mutually exclusive. Consider the random experiment of drawing a card from a standard deck of 52 playing cards. The events of drawing a heart and drawing a face card are not mutually exclusive because it is possible to draw a card that is both a heart and a face card, such as the King of Hearts.

1.1.2 Probability

1.1.2.1 Classical Definition of Probability

Definition 1.1.2.1 (Classical Probability)

The classical definition of probability states that the probability of an event is the ratio of the number of favorable outcomes to the total number of possible outcomes, provided that all outcomes are equally likely.
Let AA be an event in the sample space Ω\Omega. Let NN be the total number of exhaustive, mutually exclusive and equilikely outcomes in Ω\Omega. Let NAN_A be the number of favorable outcomes for the event AA. Then, the probability of the event AA, denoted by P(A)P(A), is given by: P(A)=NANP(A) = \frac{N_A}{N}

Note 1.1.2.1

The classical definition of probability is applicable in situations where all outcomes are equally likely, such as rolling a fair die or drawing a card from a well-shuffled deck. Furthermore, the classical definition of probability is not applicable in situations where the outcomes are not equally likely, such as in real-world scenarios where certain outcomes may be more probable than others.
The classical definition of probability also does not consider infinite sample spaces, where the number of possible outcomes is infinite. In such cases, other definitions of probability, such as the frequentist or Bayesian definitions, may be more appropriate.

Example 1.1.2.1

Let us consider the experiment of drawing a card from a standard deck of 52 playing cards. We want to find the probability of drawing an Ace.
By the classical definition, say we repeat the experiment of drawing a card from the deck a large number of times. Let NN be the total number of times we draw a card, and let NAN_A be the number of times we draw an Ace. Then, the probability of drawing an Ace is given by: P(Ace)=NANP(Ace) = \frac{N_A}{N} However, we might get different results every time we repeat the experiment if we only follow the classical definition.
For example, consider that we first do the experiment and draw cards repeatedly 50 times, out of which we get 5 Aces. Then, the probability of drawing an Ace is given by: P(Ace)=550=110P(Ace) = \frac{5}{50} = \frac{1}{10} Let’s say we repeat the experiment again and this time we draw cards repeatedly 100 times, out of which we get 20 Aces. Then, the probability of drawing an Ace is given by: P(Ace)=20100=15P(Ace) = \frac{20}{100} = \frac{1}{5} This tells us that the classical definition of probability is not very reliable, especially if the number of trials is small.

1.1.2.1.1 Conditions for Classical Definition of Probability

The classical definition of probability is based on the following conditions:

  • The sample space must be finite or at most countably infinite.

  • All outcomes in the sample space must be equally likely.

  • All events must be exhaustive that is must be subsets of Ω\Omega.

Definition 1.1.2.2 (Complement of an Event)

The complement of an event AA, denoted by ACA^C or A¯\overline{A}, is the event that includes all outcomes in the sample space Ω\Omega that are not in AA. Formally, it can be defined as: AC=Ω\AA^C = \Omega \setminus A

Example 1.1.2.2

Consider the random experiment of rolling a fair six-sided die. The sample space for this experiment is S={1,2,3,4,5,6}S = \{1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6\}. Let AA be the event of rolling an even number, that is A={2,4,6}A = \{2, 4, 6\}. The complement of the event AA, denoted by ACA^C, is the event of rolling an odd number, that is AC={1,3,5}A^C = \{1, 3, 5\}.

1.1.2.1.2 Properties of Classical Probability

We can define the probability function as follows: P:Ω→[0,1]∑i=1nP(Ai)=1\begin{align*} P: \Omega \to [0, 1] \\ \sum_{i=1}^{n} P(A_i) = 1 \end{align*} Therefore, we can write the properties of the probability set function as follows:

  • 0≤P(A)≤10 \leq P(A) \leq 1 for any event AA.

  • ∑i=1nP(Ai)=P(Ω)=1\sum_{i=1}^{n} P(A_i) = P(\Omega) =1 for a exhaustive set of mutually exclusive events.

  • P(∅)=0P(\emptyset) = 0

  • Impossible event has probability 0, since it has no favorable outcomes.

1.1.2.2 Properties of Events and their probabilities

Theorem 1.1.2.1 (Probability of union - Inclusion Exclusion Formula)

Let AA and BB be two events. Then, the probability of the union of AA and BB is given by: P(A∪B)=P(A)+P(B)−P(A∩B)P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A \cap B) For two mutually exclusive events, we have: P(A∪B)=P(A)+P(B)P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B) since P(A∩B)=0P(A \cap B) = 0.

Proof

We give a proof using Venn diagrams.

We can see from the Venn diagram that the union of two events AA and BB can be expressed as: A∪B=A+B−A∩BA \cup B = A + B - A \cap B Therefore, the probability of the union of two events can be expressed as: P(A∪B)=P(A)+P(B)−P(A∩B)P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A \cap B) Hence proved. For mutually exclusive events, we have P(A∩B)=0P(A \cap B) = 0. Therefore, the result follows. ◻

Theorem 1.1.2.2 (Probability of the complement of an event)

Let AA be an event. Then, the probability of the complement of AA, denoted by ACA^C, is given by: P(AC)=1−P(A)P(A^C) = 1 - P(A)

Proof

We have A∪AC=ΩA \cup A^C = \Omega and A∩AC=∅A \cap A^C = \emptyset. Therefore, by the previous theorem, we have: P(A∪AC)=P(A)+P(AC)−P(A∩AC)P(A \cup A^C) = P(A) + P(A^C) - P(A \cap A^C) ⟹1=P(A)+P(AC)−0\implies 1 = P(A) + P(A^C) - 0 ⟹P(AC)=1−P(A)\implies P(A^C) = 1 - P(A) Hence proved. ◻

Theorem 1.1.2.3 (Probability of a subset of an event)

Let AA be an event and BB be a subset of AA. Then, the probability of BB is given by: P(B)≤P(A)P(B) \leq P(A)

Proof

We have B⊆AB \subseteq A. Then -
A=B∪(A∩BC)A = B \cup (A \cap B^C) ⟹P(A)=P(B)+P(A∩BC)≥P(B)\implies P(A) = P(B) + P(A \cap B^C) \geq P(B) Hence proved. ◻

Theorem 1.1.2.4 (Boole’s Inequality)

Let A1,A2,…,AnA_1, A_2, \ldots, A_n be a finite collection of events. Then, the probability of the union of these events is bounded by: P(⋃i=1nAi)≤∑i=1nP(Ai)P\left( \bigcup_{i=1}^{n}A_i \right) \leq \sum_{i=1}^{n} P(A_i)

Proof

We prove the above through induction. Base Case: For n=1n=1, we have: P(A1)≤P(A1)P(A_1) \leq P(A_1) Induction Hypothesis: Assume that the result holds for n=kn=k, that is: P(⋃i=1kAi)≤∑i=1kP(Ai)P\left( \bigcup_{i=1}^{k}A_i \right) \leq \sum_{i=1}^{k} P(A_i) Induction Step: For n=k+1n=k+1, we have: P(⋃i=1k+1Ai)=P(⋃i=1kAi∪Ak+1)≤P(⋃i=1kAi)+P(Ak+1)≤∑i=1kP(Ai)+P(Ak+1)=∑i=1k+1P(Ai)\begin{align*} P\left( \bigcup_{i=1}^{k+1}A_i \right) &= P\left( \bigcup_{i=1}^{k}A_i \cup A_{k+1} \right) \\ &\leq P\left( \bigcup_{i=1}^{k}A_i \right) + P(A_{k+1}) \\ &\leq \sum_{i=1}^{k} P(A_i) + P(A_{k+1}) \\ &= \sum_{i=1}^{k+1} P(A_i) \end{align*} Hence proved. ◻

1.1.2.3 Conditional Probability

Definition 1.1.2.3 (Conditional Probability)

The conditional probability of an event AA given an event BB is defined as: P(A|B)=P(A∩B)P(B)P(A|B) = \frac{P(A \cap B)}{P(B)} for P(B)>0P(B) > 0.

Example 1.1.2.3

Let AA be the event that it rains today, and let BB be the event that I carry an umbrella. Then, the conditional probability P(A|B)P(A|B) is the probability that it rains today given that I carry an umbrella.

Example 1.1.2.4

Let AA be the event that I study for the exam, and let BB be the event that I pass the exam. Then, the conditional probability P(A|B)P(A|B) is the probability that I study for the exam given that I pass the exam.

Theorem 1.1.2.5 (Generalized Bayes’ Theorem)

Let A1,A2,…,AnA_1, A_2, \ldots, A_n be a partition of the sample space Ω\Omega, and let BB be any event such that P(B)>0P(B) > 0. Then, the conditional probability of an event AiA_i given BB is given by: P(Ai|B)=P(B|Ai)P(Ai)∑j=1nP(B|Aj)P(Aj)P(A_i|B) = \frac{P(B|A_i)P(A_i)}{\sum_{j=1}^{n} P(B|A_j)P(A_j)}

Proof

We have: P(Ai|B)=P(B|Ai)P(Ai)P(B)P(A_i|B) = \frac{P(B|A_i)P(A_i)}{P(B)} By the law of total probability, we can express P(B)P(B) as: P(B)=∑j=1nP(B|Aj)P(Aj)P(B) = \sum_{j=1}^{n} P(B|A_j)P(A_j) Therefore, we can rewrite the conditional probability as: P(Ai|B)=P(B|Ai)P(Ai)∑j=1nP(B|Aj)P(Aj)P(A_i|B) = \frac{P(B|A_i)P(A_i)}{\sum_{j=1}^{n} P(B|A_j)P(A_j)} ◻

1.1.2.4 Independence of Events

Definition 1.1.2.4 (Independent Events)

Two events AA and BB are said to be independent if the occurrence of one event does not affect the occurrence of the other event. Formally, two events AA and BB are independent if: P(A∩B)=P(A)P(B)P(A \cap B) = P(A)P(B) Equivalently, we can say that two events AA and BB are independent if: P(A|B)=P(A)andP(B|A)=P(B)P(A|B) = P(A) \quad \text{and} \quad P(B|A) = P(B) provided that P(B)>0P(B) > 0 and P(A)>0P(A) > 0.

Example 1.1.2.5

Consider the random experiment of rolling a fair six-sided die twice. Let AA be the event that the first roll is even, and let BB be the event that the second roll is odd. Then, AA and BB are independent events, since the outcome of the first roll does not affect the outcome of the second roll.

Example 1.1.2.6

Consider the random experiment of drawing 4 subsequent cards from a standard deck of 52 cards without replacement. Let AA be the event that the first card is a heart, and let BB be the event that the second card is a heart. Then, AA and BB are not independent events, since the outcome of the first draw affects the outcome of the second draw.

Definition 1.1.2.5 (Pairwise and Mutual Independence)

Pairwise Independence: A collection of events {A1,A2,…,An}\{A_1, A_2, \ldots, A_n\} is said to be pairwise independent if every pair of events is independent. That is, for all i≠ji \neq j: P(Ai∩Aj)=P(Ai)P(Aj)P(A_i \cap A_j) = P(A_i)P(A_j) Mutual Independence: A collection of events is said to be mutually independent if every event is independent of the intersection of all the others. That is, for all k≤nk \leq n: P(A1∩A2∩…∩Ak)=P(A1)P(A2)…P(Ak)P(A_1 \cap A_2 \cap \ldots \cap A_k) = P(A_1)P(A_2) \ldots P(A_k)

Example 1.1.2.7

Let A={1,2,3,4}A = \{1, 2 , 3, 4\}, B={3,4,5,6}B = \{3, 4, 5, 6\}, C={4,5,6,7}C = \{4, 5, 6, 7\} be three events in the sample space Ω={1,2,3,4,5,6,7,8}\Omega = \{1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8\}. Then, we can see that AA, BB and CC are independent when considered together, but not pairwise independent. Let us verify this -
P(A)=P(B)=P(C)=48=12P(A) = P(B) = P(C) = \frac{4}{8} = \frac{1}{2}
P(A∩B)=P({3,4})=28=14=P(A)P(B)P(A\cap B) = P(\{3, 4\}) = \frac{2}{8} = \frac{1}{4} = P(A)P(B)
P(A∩C)=P({4})=18≠P(A)P(C)P(A\cap C) = P(\{4\}) = \frac{1}{8} \neq P(A)P(C)
P(B∩C)=P({4,5,6})=38≠P(B)P(C)P(B\cap C) = P(\{4, 5, 6\}) = \frac{3}{8} \neq P(B)P(C)
However,
P(A∩B∩C)=P({4})=18=P(A)P(B)P(C)P(A\cap B \cap C) = P(\{4\}) = \frac{1}{8} = P(A)P(B)P(C)
Therefore, the triple product holds while pairwise independence fails.

Note 1.1.2.3

It is important to note that pairwise independence does not imply mutual independence. That is, even if every pair of events in a collection is independent, it does not necessarily mean that the entire collection is mutually independent. Mutual independence is a stronger condition than pairwise independence, and it requires that every event in the collection be independent of the intersection of all the others.

Definition 1.1.2.6 (Conditional Independence)

Two events AA and BB are said to be conditionally independent given a third event CC if the occurrence of AA does not affect the occurrence of BB when CC has occurred. Formally, AA and BB are conditionally independent given CC if: P(A∩B|C)=P(A|C)P(B|C)P(A \cap B | C) = P(A|C)P(B|C) provided that P(C)>0P(C) > 0.

Note 1.1.2.4

Note that two events being conditionally independent given a third event does not imply that they are independent in general. Conditional independence is a weaker condition than unconditional independence.

1.1.3 Different Approaches and Definitions of Probability

1.1.3.1 Statistical Definition of Probability

Definition 1.1.3.1 (Statistical definition of Probability)

The statistical definition of probability states that the probability of an event is the limit of its relative frequency in a large number of trials.
Let AA be an event in the sample space Ω\Omega. Let NN be the total number of trials of the random experiment, and let NAN_A be the number of times the event AA occurs in these trials. Then, the probability of the event AA, denoted by P(A)P(A), is given by: P(A)=limN→∞NANP(A) = \lim_{N \to \infty} \frac{N_A}{N} provided that the limit exists.

Note 1.1.3.1

This definition provides a more reliable foundation for the concept of probability, since with this definition, we will always get the same probability for any event in a sample space. Moreover, it emphasizes the importance of conducting a large number of trials to obtain a stable estimate of probability. We shall prove under the central limit theorem that the distribution of the sample mean approaches a normal distribution as the sample size increases, regardless of the original distribution of the data.

Example 1.1.3.1

Consider the random experiment of flipping a fair coin multiple times. Let AA be the event that we get heads on the first flip, and let BB be the event that we get heads on the second flip. According to the statistical definition of probability, we can estimate P(A)P(A) and P(B)P(B) by conducting a large number of trials and observing the relative frequencies of heads. It can be empirically verified that the events AA and BB are independent, since the outcome of the first flip does not affect the outcome of the second flip.

1.1.3.2 Axiomatic Definition of Probability

Definition 1.1.3.2 (σ\sigma-field or σ\sigma-algebra)

A σ\sigma-field (or σ\sigma-algebra) on a set Ω\Omega is a collection ℱ\mathcal{F} of subsets of Ω\Omega such that:

  1. The empty set is in ℱ\mathcal{F}: ∅∈ℱ\emptyset \in \mathcal{F}.

  2. If A∈ℱA \in \mathcal{F}, then its complement is also in ℱ\mathcal{F}: Ac∈ℱA^c \in \mathcal{F}.

  3. If A1,A2,…∈ℱA_1, A_2, \ldots \in \mathcal{F} are countably many sets, then their union is also in ℱ\mathcal{F}: ⋃i=1∞Ai∈ℱ\bigcup_{i=1}^{\infty} A_i \in \mathcal{F}.

In essence, a σ\sigma-field is closed under complementation and countable unions.

Example 1.1.3.2

Consider the sample space Ω\Omega of all possible outcomes when rolling a fair six-sided die. The σ\sigma-algebra ℱ\mathcal{F} generated by this sample space includes all possible subsets of Ω\Omega, including the empty set, singletons (e.g., {1}\{1\}, {2}\{2\}), and the entire sample space itself. This σ\sigma-algebra is closed under complementation and countable unions.

In general, we note that the concept of a σ\sigma-field is fundamental to the axiomatic approach to probability. Every power set of a sample space forms a σ\sigma-field. We define here the smallest and largest σ\sigma-field on a sample space Ω\Omega:

  • The smallest σ\sigma-field is the trivial σ\sigma-field, which contains only the empty set and the entire sample space: ℱmin={∅,Ω}\mathcal{F}_{\text{min}} = \{\emptyset, \Omega\}.

  • The largest σ\sigma-field is the power set of Ω\Omega, which contains all possible subsets of Ω\Omega: ℱmax=2Ω\mathcal{F}_{\text{max}} = 2^{\Omega}.

Definition 1.1.3.3 (Kalmagarov’s Definition)

Let Ω\Omega be a sample space, and let ℱ\mathcal{F} be a σ\sigma-algebra of subsets of Ω\Omega. A probability measure PP is a function that assigns a probability to each event in ℱ\mathcal{F} such that the following axioms hold:

  1. Non-negativity: For any event A∈ℱA \in \mathcal{F}, P(A)≥0P(A) \geq 0.

  2. Normalization: P(Ω)=1P(\Omega) = 1.

  3. Countable additivity: For any countable collection of disjoint events A1,A2,…∈ℱA_1, A_2, \ldots \in \mathcal{F}, we have: P(⋃i=1∞Ai)=∑i=1∞P(Ai)P\left(\bigcup_{i=1}^{\infty} A_i\right) = \sum_{i=1}^{\infty} P(A_i)

This probability function on ℱ\mathcal{F} satisfies the axioms of probability and provides a rigorous foundation for the concept of probability in the context of measure theory. Further, this function is unique.

Example 1.1.3.3

Let Ω\Omega be the sample space of rolling a fair six-sided die. The σ\sigma-algebra ℱ\mathcal{F} generated by this sample space includes all possible subsets of Ω\Omega. A probability measure PP can be defined on ℱ\mathcal{F} by assigning a probability of 16\frac{1}{6} to each individual outcome (e.g., P({1})=16P(\{1\}) = \frac{1}{6}, P({2})=16P(\{2\}) = \frac{1}{6}, etc.) and ensuring that the total probability sums to 1. Note that this construction satisfies all the axioms of a probability measure.

1.1.4 Random Variables and Probability Distributions

1.1.4.1 Random Variables

Definition 1.1.4.1 (Random Variable)

A random variable is a function that assigns a real number to each outcome in a sample space of a random experiment. Formally, a random variable XX is a function from the sample space Ω\Omega to the real numbers ℝ\mathbb{R}: X:Ω→ℝX: \Omega \to \mathbb{R} Random variables can be classified into two main types:

  • Discrete Random Variables: These take on a countable number of distinct values. Examples include the outcome of rolling a die or the number of heads in a series of coin flips.

  • Continuous Random Variables: These can take on any value within a given range or interval. Examples include the height of individuals in a population or the time it takes to complete a task.

1.2.2.1 Joint PMF and Distributions

Definition 1.2.2.1 (Joint Probability Mass Function (PMF))

The joint probability mass function (PMF) of two discrete random variables XX and YY is a function that gives the probability that XX takes on a specific value xx and YY takes on a specific value yy. It is denoted as P(X=x,Y=y)P(X = x, Y = y) or simply P(x,y)P(x, y). The joint PMF must satisfy the following properties:

  • Non-negativity: P(x,y)≥0P(x, y) \geq 0 for all possible values of xx and yy.

  • Normalization: The sum of the joint probabilities over all possible values of xx and yy must equal 1. That is, ∑x∑yP(x,y)=1\sum_{x} \sum_{y} P(x, y) = 1.

  • Marginalization: The marginal PMFs of XX and YY can be obtained by summing the joint PMF over the other variable. That is, P(X=x)=∑yP(x,y)P(X = x) = \sum_{y} P(x, y) and P(Y=y)=∑xP(x,y)P(Y = y) = \sum_{x} P(x, y).

1.2.3 Geometric Distribution

1.2.3.1 Definition and Properties

A geometric distribution is a discrete probability distribution that models the number of trials needed to achieve the first success in a sequence of independent Bernoulli trials, where each trial has the same probability of success. 2.3.2 Basic Measures of Dispersion

2.3.2.1 Range

Definition 2.3.2.1 (Range)

The range of a data set is the difference between the maximum and minimum values in the data set. It is a measure of dispersion that indicates how spread out the values are. The range is calculated as: Range=Maximum Value−Minimum Value\text{Range} = \text{Maximum Value} - \text{Minimum Value}

Note 2.3.2.1

The range is a simple and easy-to-calculate measure of dispersion, but it has some limitations. It is sensitive to extreme values (outliers) in the data set, which can significantly affect the range. Additionally, the range does not provide information about the distribution of values within the data set, as it only considers the two extreme values. Therefore, while the range can be useful for a quick assessment of dispersion, it is often supplemented with other measures of dispersion, such as variance and standard deviation, for a more comprehensive understanding of the data set.

2.3.2.2 Mean Deviation

Definition 2.3.2.2 (Mean Deviation)

The mean deviation (also known as the average absolute deviation) of a data set is a measure of dispersion that quantifies the average distance of each data point from the mean of the data set. It is calculated as the average of the absolute differences between each data point and the mean. The formula for mean deviation is given by: Mean Deviation=1n∑i=1n|xi−x‾|\text{Mean Deviation} = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} |x_i - \bar{x}| where nn is the number of data points, xix_i represents each individual data point, and x‾\bar{x} is the mean of the data set.

Note 2.3.2.2

The mean deviation provides a measure of how much the data points deviate from the mean on average. It is a useful measure of dispersion, especially when the data set contains outliers, as it is less sensitive to extreme values compared to other measures like variance and standard deviation. However, mean deviation does not have some of the mathematical properties that make variance and standard deviation more widely used in statistical analysis, such as being based on squared deviations.
