Theorem 1

Proof

Assume the contrary that x>yx > y. Then, we have: x−y>0x - y > 0 Let ϵ=x−y2\epsilon = \frac{x - y}{2}. Note that ϵ>0\epsilon > 0. Hence, we conclude that our assumption is false. Therefore, we have x≤yx \leq y. ◻

Exercise 2

Formulate -

  1. A definition of a lower bound of a non-empty set E⊂ℝE \subset \mathbb{R}

  2. A definition for the greatest lower bound of a non-empty bounded below subset EE of ℝ\mathbb{R}.

Proof

  1. A number m∈ℝm \in \mathbb{R} is called a lower bound of a non-empty set E⊂ℝE \subset \mathbb{R} if for every x∈Ex \in E, we have m≤xm \leq x.

  2. A number g∈ℝg \in \mathbb{R} is called the greatest lower bound (or infimum) of a non-empty bounded below subset EE of ℝ\mathbb{R} if:

    • gg is a lower bound of EE, i.e., for every x∈Ex \in E, we have g≤xg \leq x.

    • For any other lower bound mm of EE, we have m≤gm \leq g. In other words, gg is the largest among all lower bounds of EE.

We can alternatively define the greatest lower bound using the following property: A number g∈ℝg \in \mathbb{R} is the greatest lower bound of EE if for every ϵ>0\epsilon > 0, there exists an element xϵ∈Ex_\epsilon \in E such that g≤xϵ<g+ϵg \leq x_\epsilon < g + \epsilon. ◻

Exercise 3

Show that every non-empty bounded below subset of the real numbers admit a greatest lower bound. The greatest lower bound of a non-empty bounded below set EE is denoted by inf⁡E\inf E.

Proof

Let A⊂ℝA \subset \mathbb{R} be a non-empty bounded below subset. Then it follows that - ∃m∈ℝ:m≤x,∀x∈A\exists m \in \mathbb{R}: m \leq x, \forall x \in A Define the set B={−x:x∈A}B = \{-x : x \in A\}. Since AA is bounded below, it follows that BB is bounded above by −m-m - m≤x⟹−x≤−m⟹−m is an upper bound of Bm \leq x \implies -x \leq -m \implies -m \text{ is an upper bound of } B Since AA is non-empty, BB is also non-empty. By the completeness property of real numbers, every non-empty bounded above subset of ℝ\mathbb{R} has a least upper bound. Therefore, BB has a least upper bound, say s=sup⁡Bs = \sup B. We claim that −s-s is the greatest lower bound of AA. To see this, we need to show two things:

  • −s-s is a lower bound of AA.

  • For any other lower bound mm of AA, we have m≤−sm \leq -s.

Proof that −s-s is a lower bound of AA: Let x∈Ax \in A. Then −x∈B-x \in B. Since ss is an upper bound of BB, we have −x≤s-x \leq s. This implies that x≥−sx \geq -s. Since this holds for all x∈Ax \in A, it follows that −s-s is a lower bound of AA.
Proof that −s-s is the greatest lower bound of AA: Let mm be any lower bound of AA. Then for every x∈Ax \in A, we have m≤xm \leq x. This implies that for every y∈By \in B (where y=−xy = -x for some x∈Ax \in A), we have −y≤−m-y \leq -m, or equivalently, y≥−my \geq -m. Therefore, −m-m is an upper bound of BB. Since ss is the least upper bound of BB, we have s≤−ms \leq -m. This implies that −s≥m-s \geq m. Since this holds for any lower bound mm of AA, it follows that −s-s is the greatest lower bound of AA. Thus, we conclude that every non-empty bounded below subset of the real numbers admits a greatest lower bound. ◻

Let A,BA, B be non-empty subsets of ℝ\mathbb{R} such that a≤ba \leq b for all a∈Aa \in A and b∈Bb \in B. Let A be bounded above and B be bounded below. Show that sup⁡A≤inf⁡B\sup A \leq \inf B.

Proof

Let s=sup⁡As = \sup A and i=inf⁡Bi = \inf B. We need to show that s≤is \leq i. Since ss is an upper bound of AA, we have a≤sa \leq s for all a∈Aa \in A. Then, by the definition of infimum, we have - ∀ϵ>0,∃bϵ∈B:i≤bϵ<i+ϵ\forall \epsilon > 0, \exists b_\epsilon \in B: i \leq b_\epsilon < i + \epsilon Let us take ϵ=s−i\epsilon = s - i. This implies that bϵb_\epsilon is an upper bound of AA. Since ss is the least upper bound of AA, we have s≤bϵs \leq b_\epsilon. This is a contradiction since we have bϵ<sb_\epsilon < s. Hence, we conclude that our assumption that s>is > i is false. Therefore, we have s≤is \leq i, or equivalently, sup⁡A≤inf⁡B\sup A \leq \inf B. ◻

Let A,BA, B be non-empty bounded subsets of ℝ\mathbb{R}. Let A+B={a+b:a∈A,b∈B}A + B = \{a + b : a \in A, b \in B\}. Show that sup⁡(A+B)=sup⁡A+sup⁡B\sup (A + B) = \sup A + \sup B.

Proof

Let sA=sup⁡As_A = \sup A and sB=sup⁡Bs_B = \sup B. We need to show that sup⁡(A+B)=sA+sB\sup (A + B) = s_A + s_B. First, we show that sA+sBs_A + s_B is an upper bound of A+BA + B. Let x∈A+Bx \in A + B. Then, by definition, there exist a∈Aa \in A and b∈Bb \in B such that x=a+bx = a + b. Since sAs_A is an upper bound of AA, we have a≤sAa \leq s_A. Similarly, since sBs_B is an upper bound of BB, we have b≤sBb \leq s_B. Adding these two inequalities, we get: x=a+b≤sA+sBx = a + b \leq s_A + s_B This shows that sA+sBs_A + s_B is an upper bound of A+BA + B.
Now, we need to show that sA+sBs_A + s_B is the least upper bound of A+BA + B. We write the definition of supremums for AA and BB:

  1. For every ϵ>0\epsilon > 0, there exists aϵ∈Aa_\epsilon \in A such that sA−ϵ2<aϵ≤sA\begin{equation} s_A - \frac{\epsilon}{2} < a_\epsilon \leq s_A \end{equation}

Adding (1) and (2), we get: sA+sB−ϵ<aϵ+bϵ≤sA+sBs_A + s_B - \epsilon < a_\epsilon + b_\epsilon \leq s_A + s_B Note that aϵ+bϵ∈A+Ba_\epsilon + b_\epsilon \in A + B.
